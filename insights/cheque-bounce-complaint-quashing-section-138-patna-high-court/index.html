Cheque Bounce Case under Section 138: When the High Court Can Quash It, Explained Simply

By Pankaj Kumar Choudhary, Advocate, Patna High Court · Choudhary Law Chambers, Patna · 19 November 2026

Cheque bounce complaints under Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881 are among the most common criminal cases in Bihar's courts. Sometimes a complaint is filed against people who had nothing to do with the cheque, or the legal steps were not followed. This guide explains, in simple words, when such a complaint can be quashed.

First, learn these words

  • Section 138: the offence of dishonour of a cheque for insufficient funds, when the drawer does not pay after a legal notice.
  • Section 141: when the cheque is issued by a company or firm, persons in charge of and responsible for its business can also be made accused.
  • Legal notice: the written demand the payee must send after the cheque bounces.
  • Quashing: the High Court ending the complaint, using its inherent power under Section 528 of the BNSS, 2023.

The steps the law requires

In simple words, Section 138 requires that the cheque was presented within its validity, the payee sent a written notice within 30 days of learning of the dishonour, and the drawer did not pay within 15 days of receiving the notice. Under Section 142, the complaint must be filed in writing by the payee within one month after the cause of action arises.

Grounds the High Court looks at

  • Director not in charge: in S.M.S. Pharmaceuticals Ltd. v. Neeta Bhalla (2005) 8 SCC 89, the Supreme Court held that the complaint must specifically state that a director was in charge of and responsible for the company's business when the offence was committed. A bare designation is not enough.
  • Complaint filed too early: in Yogendra Pratap Singh v. Savitri Pandey (2014) 10 SCC 713, the Supreme Court held that a complaint filed before the 15-day period after notice has ended is not maintainable.
  • Legal requirement missing on the face of the record: for example, no notice at all.

Questions such as "there was no debt" or "the cheque was only security" usually depend on evidence and are generally decided at trial, not in a quashing petition.

Settlement is possible

The offence under Section 138 is compoundable under Section 147 of the NI Act. If the parties settle, the case can be closed in the trial court on compromise.

An imaginary example

This is an imaginary example to explain the procedure; it is not a real case.

A cheque issued by a Patna company bounces. The complaint names the company, its managing director, and Neha (an imaginary name), a non-executive director who only attends board meetings. The complaint does not say what role Neha had in the company's day-to-day business. Neha files a petition under Section 528 BNSS in the Patna High Court with the complaint, the summoning order, and company records showing her position.

Documents to keep ready

  • Complaint and summoning order (orders can be seen on eCourts Services)
  • Copy of the cheque, return memo and legal notice
  • Proof of the date the notice was received
  • For directors: company records showing your role

Recovery cases are also covered on our money recovery and cheque bounce page. Case status can be tracked on the Patna High Court website.

Frequently asked questions

Can a cheque bounce case be quashed?

Yes, in limited situations, such as a director not shown to be in charge of the business, a complaint filed before the notice period ended, or a missing legal requirement on the face of the record.

I am a director but did not sign the cheque. Am I liable?

Only if the complaint shows you were in charge of and responsible for the company's business at the time. This is checked on the facts.

Can the case end by settlement?

Yes. The offence is compoundable under Section 147 of the NI Act.

Will the High Court decide whether the debt existed?

Usually not. That depends on evidence and is generally decided at trial.
