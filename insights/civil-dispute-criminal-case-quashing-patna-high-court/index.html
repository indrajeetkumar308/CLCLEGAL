A Money or Land Dispute Turned into a Criminal Case? When It Can Be Quashed

By Pankaj Kumar Choudhary, Advocate, Patna High Court · Choudhary Law Chambers, Patna · 19 November 2026

In Bihar, a failed land deal, an unpaid loan or a business quarrel often reaches the police as an FIR for cheating or breach of trust. Sometimes there is a real crime. Sometimes it is only a civil dispute, filed as a criminal case to put pressure. This guide explains, in simple words, how the High Court tells the two apart.

In this article
  1. First, learn these words
  2. What the Supreme Court has said
  3. What the High Court checks
  4. An imaginary example
  5. Documents to keep ready
  6. Frequently asked questions

First, learn these words

  • Civil dispute: a disagreement about money, property or a contract, decided by a civil suit.
  • Cheating (BNS 318): deceiving a person dishonestly to make him hand over property (old IPC 420). See the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023.
  • Criminal breach of trust (BNS 316): dishonestly misusing property entrusted to a person (old IPC 406).
  • Quashing: the High Court ending the case under Section 528 of the BNSS, 2023 (old CrPC 482), as shown in the CrPC–BNSS comparison table.

What the Supreme Court has said

  • In Indian Oil Corporation v. NEPC India Ltd. (2006) 6 SCC 736, the Supreme Court disapproved of the practice of converting purely civil disputes into criminal cases.
  • In Paramjeet Batra v. State of Uttarakhand (2013) 11 SCC 673, it held that where a dispute is essentially civil and has been given a cloak of a criminal offence, the High Court should not hesitate to quash the proceedings.
  • In Hridaya Ranjan Prasad Verma v. State of Bihar (2000) 4 SCC 168, a case from Bihar, it explained that for cheating, a dishonest intention must exist at the time of the promise. A mere failure to keep a promise later is not cheating.

What the High Court checks

  • Does the FIR, read as it is, show dishonest intention from the beginning?
  • Or does it only show a breach of an agreement, a delay in payment, or a dispute about accounts?
  • Is a civil suit already pending on the same facts? This alone does not end a criminal case, but it is relevant.

The High Court does not hold a trial. If the FIR shows the ingredients of an offence, the case usually continues. General grounds for quashing are explained in FIR quashing under Section 528 BNSS.

An imaginary example

This is an imaginary example to explain the procedure; it is not a real case.

Ramesh (an imaginary name) agrees to sell land in Danapur to Vinod and takes an advance. A family member then objects and the sale stalls. Vinod files an FIR for cheating against Ramesh. Ramesh shows the written agreement, the family objection, and his offer to return the advance. He files a petition under Section 528 BNSS in the Patna High Court, saying the dispute is about an agreement for sale and belongs in a civil court.

Documents to keep ready

  • FIR and chargesheet, if filed
  • The agreement, receipts, bank statements and messages between the parties
  • Papers of any civil suit on the same facts
  • Certified copies of court orders (see eCourts Services)

Case status can be tracked on the Patna High Court website.

Frequently asked questions

Is every failed deal a cheating case?

No. Cheating needs dishonest intention at the time of the promise. A later breach of an agreement is generally a civil matter.

A civil suit is already pending. Does the FIR end?

Not automatically. The pendency of a civil suit is relevant, but the court checks whether the FIR shows the ingredients of an offence.

Where is the quashing petition filed?

In the Patna High Court, under Section 528 BNSS, for cases from any district of Bihar.

Will the High Court examine evidence?

No full trial is held. The court reads the FIR and the papers as they are.
