Received Summons in a Complaint Case? How It Can Be Challenged, Explained Simply

By Pankaj Kumar Choudhary, Advocate, Patna High Court · Choudhary Law Chambers, Patna · 19 November 2026

Not every criminal case starts with an FIR. A person can file a complaint directly before the Magistrate, who may then summon the accused. Many people first learn of such a case when a summons arrives. This guide explains, in simple words, what the law requires before a summons is issued and how it can be challenged.

In this article

  2. The steps under the BNSS

First, learn these words

  • Complaint case: a case started by a complaint to the Magistrate, not by an FIR.
  • Cognizance: the court formally taking up the case.
  • Summons (issue of process): the order calling the accused to appear.
  • Revision: asking a higher court to examine the legality of the order.

The steps under the BNSS

Under the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (BNSS):

  • Section 223: the Magistrate examines the complainant and witnesses. A new proviso says that no cognizance shall be taken without giving the accused an opportunity of being heard (this did not exist in old CrPC Section 200).
  • Section 225: the Magistrate may postpone the issue of process and inquire or direct investigation. Where the accused lives outside the Magistrate's area, this inquiry is mandatory (old CrPC 202).
  • Section 226: dismissal of the complaint if there is no sufficient ground (old CrPC 203).
  • Section 227: issue of summons if there is sufficient ground (old CrPC 204).

The old and new section numbers are taken from the CrPC–BNSS comparison table of the Government of Tripura.

What the Supreme Court has said

  • In Pepsi Foods Ltd. v. Special Judicial Magistrate (1998) 5 SCC 749, the Supreme Court held that summoning an accused is a serious matter, and the order must show that the Magistrate applied his mind to the facts and the law.
  • In Vijay Dhanuka v. Najima Mamtaj (2014) 14 SCC 638, it held that the inquiry before summoning an accused who lives outside the Magistrate's area is mandatory.

Two routes to challenge

  • Criminal revision against the summoning order, in the Sessions Court or the High Court (Sections 438 and 442 BNSS, old CrPC 397 and 401).
  • Petition under Section 528 BNSS in the Patna High Court to quash the complaint and the summoning order. See quashing an FIR or complaint case.

Which route is right depends on the order and the facts of the case.

An imaginary example

This is an imaginary example to explain the procedure; it is not a real case.

Anil (an imaginary name) lives in Kolkata. A Magistrate in Patna summons him on a complaint by a former business associate. The order sheet shows no inquiry before the summons, and Anil was not given a chance to be heard before cognizance. He obtains certified copies of the complaint and the order sheet and files a challenge in the Patna High Court on these grounds.

Documents to keep ready

  • The summons, and a certified copy of the complaint
  • Certified copy of the order sheet, showing what happened before the summons (see eCourts Services)
  • Proof of your address, if you live outside the Magistrate's area
  • Any document that answers the allegations on the face of the record

Case status can be tracked on the Patna High Court website.

Frequently asked questions

Is the accused heard before cognizance in a complaint case?

Under the new proviso to Section 223 BNSS, no cognizance shall be taken without giving the accused an opportunity of being heard.

I live in another district or state. Does that matter?

Yes. Where the accused lives outside the Magistrate's area, an inquiry under Section 225 BNSS before summoning is mandatory.

How can a summoning order be challenged?

By criminal revision, or by a petition under Section 528 BNSS in the Patna High Court, depending on the order and the facts.

Should I ignore the summons?

No. Not appearing can lead to warrants. Take advice and appear or seek exemption as the law allows.
